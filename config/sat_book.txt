GENERAL INFORMATION ABOUT THE
DIGITAL SAT

1) Notes 
What the SAT really tests
It’s a test-taking game: it measures how well you take this test, not your worth or school knowledge.


Questions are written by professional test-writers; you can learn their patterns.


Structure & timing
Total time: 2h14m + 10-min break.


Reading & Writing (RW): 2 modules → 27 Q / 32 min each (all multiple-choice; 4 experimental Q total across the test).


Math: 2 modules → 22 Q / 35 min each (mostly multiple-choice + some fill-in).


RW content order (every module)
Craft & Structure (~28%)


Information & Ideas (~26%)


Standard English Conventions (~26%)


Expression of Ideas (~20%)


Each short passage (or pair) has just one question; some come with a small graphic.


Math content mix (scattered within each module)
Algebra (~35%), Advanced Math (~35%), PSDA/Data (~15%), Geo/Trig (~15%).


Calculator: Built-in Desmos on all math questions; approved handhelds also allowed.


Adaptive design & scoring
Your Module 1 performance sets Module 2 (Easier vs Harder).


Some questions are weighted more; total score 400–1600 (RW 200–800 + Math 200–800).


No penalty for wrong answers; don’t leave blanks.


Each module contains unmarked experimental questions—answer everything.


Logistics
Delivered in the Bluebook app at schools/test centers (saves progress; works even if Wi-Fi drops).


Scores arrive in days, and only section/total scores are reported (no subscores).


Getting started smartly
Take a diagnostic, read the category report, focus on high-impact skills first, and practice with the same calculator you’ll use on test day.





2) Short Summary
The Digital SAT is a 2-hour-14-minute adaptive test with two sections (Reading & Writing, Math), each split into two timed modules. RW uses short passages with one question each and follows a fixed order of skills; Math mixes four content areas and allows Desmos on every question. Your first module performance determines whether the second is easier or harder, and some questions carry extra weight. There’s no guessing penalty and a few unscored experimental items. Treat the SAT like a learnable system: practice the formats, use your calculator wisely, and train to maximize points on the most valuable questions.

3) Key Insights (what you should come away with)
Game mindset beats generic studying. Learn patterns and traps; don’t study everything equally.


Adaptive = targeted. Doing well early unlocks a higher ceiling; accuracy in Module 1 matters.


Weighted questions exist. Prioritize getting through and nailing the “payoff” items.


Desmos literacy is a skill. It’s a time-saver on some problems and a trap on others—practice judgment.


Zero penalty for guessing. Always fill every question; use elimination aggressively.


Section strategy differs. RW is fixed by skill blocks; Math difficulty ramps within each module.

Chapter 2 
Cracking the Digital SAT: Basic
Principles
1) Notes
Mindset: The SAT repeats patterns; learn how test-writers think. It’s a different skill from school tests.


Two-Pass Method (“Take the easy test first”): Skip early, skip often. First pass = cherry-pick quick/accurate points; second pass = come back to marked items. Don’t get stuck; if time’s low, guess so nothing is blank.


POOD (Personal Order Of Difficulty): RW has rough order by content area; Math by overall difficulty—but your order is personal. Work in the order that suits your strengths.


Mark & Move On + Review Page caution: Use Mark for Review and the Overview/Review tool to jump around and see unanswered items. On the Review Page, clicking Next ends the module—no confirm!


Guessing policy: No wrong-answer penalty; eliminate what you can and guess. Any letter is fine (roughly even distribution).


Pace for score gains: You’re scored on correct answers, not attempts. It’s often better to slow slightly, nail your POOD items, and leave/guess the rest.


POE (Process of Elimination): Look for wrong answers first; Answer Eliminator helps track crossed-off choices; Annotate is RW-only (not answer choices/figures; not in Math).


Scratch-paper system: Next to each Q#, use ✓ (seems right), ~ (maybe), ? (don’t understand), and X (wrong). Keep it neat and consistent.


Careless-error firewall: Build habits—re-read the question, check transcription & calculations before locking an answer.


2) Short Summary
Chapter 2 gives the core playbook: treat the Digital SAT like a strategy game. Do an easy-first two-pass, guided by your POOD; mark & move to protect time; use POE (+ Answer Eliminator & Annotate) to narrow choices; guess instead of leaving blanks; and slow down to score more by avoiding careless errors.
3) Key Insights (what you should internalize)
Points > pride: Skip fast, bank easy points, return later.


Your order wins: POOD beats official order; play to strengths.


No blanks: Eliminate → guess; distribution is roughly even.


Slow = higher score (often): Accuracy outruns rushing.


Tools matter: Review/Mark, Answer Eliminator, Annotate (RW-only), neat scratch paper.


Avoid careless errors: Build a 10-second double-check habit.

6) Some common Patterns/traps
a) RW: The Extreme-Word Trap (wrong answer pattern)
What it is: A tempting choice uses absolute language that the passage never supports.
 Red flags: always, never, completely, only, proves, must, everyone, none.
 Mini-example:
 Passage: “Some researchers suggest the method may help in certain cases.”
 Wrong choice: “The method always cures patients.”
 Better choice: “The method may help some patients.”
 Teach the fix: Match the scope and tone of the passage. Circle hedging words (may, can, sometimes, tends) and reject answers that crank them up to absolutes.
b) Math: Linear vs Exponential Growth (misread pattern)
What it is: Students mix up “adds the same amount each year” (linear) with “increases by the same percent” (exponential).
 Cues:
Linear → “+ 5 points each month,” “decreases by 12 units per year,” form: y=mx+by = mx + by=mx+b.


Exponential → “grows by 8% each year,” “multiplies by 1.08,” form: y=a(r)ty = a(r)^ty=a(r)t.
 Mini-example: Start = 100. After 3 years…


Linear +15/year → 100+3⋅15=145100 + 3\cdot 15 = 145100+3⋅15=145


Exponential +15%/year → 100(1.15)3≈152100(1.15)^3 \approx 152100(1.15)3≈152
 Teach the fix: Underline the keyword (amount vs percent). Write a quick model before computing. If it’s percent, multiply; if it’s amount, add.



Bonus one-liners (optional, quick to share)
RW: Half-Right, Whole-Wrong. Choice quotes a true detail but adds an unsupported claim. If any part isn’t backed by the text, toss it.


Math: Average-Speed Trap. You can’t average speeds; compute total distance ÷ total time (round-trip equal distances ≠ mean of mph).































Chapter 3 Reading and Writing Introduction
1) Notes 

Structure & timing: RW has 2 modules, 27 Q / 32 min each (total 54). About 25–33 Reading and 19–31 Writing across both modules.


Reading vs Writing (what they test): Reading = meaning/purpose/inference; Writing = punctuation, grammar, style, transitions/rhetorical fit.


Three buckets to recognize (helps POOD): Think of each module as Reading → Rules → Rhetoric (in that order & rough proportion). Start where you’re strongest.


Reading bucket—how to spot: Poems/fiction, dual texts, graphs, or fill-in-the-blank word questions live here; many ask for meaning/purpose.


Rules bucket—how to spot: They all use the same stem: “Which choice completes the text so that it conforms to the conventions of Standard English?” Often among the fastest items.


Rhetoric bucket—how to spot: Two formats: transitions and bullet-point items (no Rules stem).


How to start each question: Read the question first → identify bucket → run that playbook. This also tells you what to hunt for when you read the text.


POOD reminder: If you’re stronger at Writing, it can make sense to start mid-module where those appear; if Reading is easier, start at the top.


2) Short Summary
RW = two 32-minute modules (27 Q each). Treat each module as Reading → Rules → Rhetoric. Spot the type quickly: poems/dual texts/graphs/fill-in-word = Reading; fixed Standard English stem = Rules; transitions/bullet-points = Rhetoric. Start with the question, pick the bucket, and apply the right steps—following your POOD to begin where you’ll score fastest.
3) Key Insights (what to internalize)
Bucket first, passage second. Identifying Reading / Rules / Rhetoric up front saves time and errors.


Rules stem = green flag. The fixed wording lets you scan-jump directly to quick grammar/punctuation wins.


Rhetoric is predictable. If it’s a transition or bullet list, switch to the rhetoric playbook.


Play your POOD. Start where you’re strongest (often mid-module for Writing-strong testers).

Chapter 4 The Reading Questions

1) Notes
What you face: Reading is just over half of RW and always comes before Writing; each passage is 25–150 words with one question—so efficiency matters.


Open-book mindset: RW Reading is about finding support in the text; there’s one right answer based on the passage(s).


Mission: Identify the task quickly and locate support efficiently.


POOD for Reading: If a passage/type looks slow, mark & move, then come back; consider type, literature, and topic to decide order; always guess on skipped items.


Eight Reading types (fixed order): Vocabulary → Purpose → Dual Texts → Retrieval → Main Idea → Claims → Charts → Conclusions. Not all appear in every module.


Basic approach (5 steps): Question → Type → Passage → Highlight/Annotate → POE. Start from the question so you know what to hunt in the passage.


Active reading: Don’t read the passage first; know your goal before you read.


Annotate when it helps (e.g., Purpose): Jot main idea + attitude; use it to drive eliminations.


Classic traps to spot with POE: Recycled Language (re-uses words, twists idea), Beyond the Text / Half-Right (partly supported, partly not).


2) Short Summary
Chapter 4 reframes Reading as an evidence hunt: start with the question, identify the type, then read the passage only to find what solves that task. Use highlighting/annotation to anchor evidence and POE to delete trap answers (e.g., recycled language, half-right). Apply POOD—skip, mark, and return—to spend time where you score fastest.
3) Key Insights (what to internalize)
Question-first saves time: It tells you exactly what to look for when you read.


POOD is power: Order questions by your ease; mark & return instead of getting stuck.


Evidence beats vibe: Use highlight + brief annotation to anchor answers; don’t over-summarize.


POE wins close calls: Delete answers inconsistent with your highlight/annotation; be wary of recycled wording.


Know the menu: The eight types arrive in a stable order—use that predictability.

Chapter 5: Reading Question Types 1–3: Craft
and Structure


1) Notes 
Scope of this chapter
You’ll tackle three RW types about how passages are built: Vocabulary, Purpose/Function/Structure, and Dual Texts (Text 1 + Text 2).


Vocabulary (two formats + playbook)
Formats to spot: “most logical and precise word/phrase” (fill-in) and “As used in the text, … most nearly means” (in-context).


Steps: treat the target as a blank → read sentence + nearby lines → highlight the context clue → write your own word → use POE.


Common traps: Beyond the Text, Extreme Language, Opposite.


Smart study: use the book’s Greatest Hits, build your own list, and read one challenging article/story daily; don’t over-memorize rare, archaic lit words.


Purpose / Function / Structure (playbook)
What they ask: why the author wrote it (purpose), what a sentence is doing (function), how the passage is organized (structure). Evidence-based, not speculation.


Steps: read the question → if you see purpose/function/structure, it’s this type → read the sentence and the line before/after → highlight the connection → annotate the role → POE.




Dual Texts (playbook)
What they ask: how Text 2 responds to a claim/idea in Text 1 (agree / disagree / extend).


Steps: read stem → highlight the claim in Text 1 → highlight Text 2’s stance on that same claim → annotate the relationship (agree/disagree/extend) → POE. Traps = Opposites & Extreme. Use POOD if time-heavy.


Worked example: Text 2 presents handwashing as a possible alternative (not a total replacement) → keep moderate wording, reject Extreme/Recycled answers.



2) Short Summary
Chapter 5 teaches three RW skills: pick precise Vocabulary using context and POE; find Purpose/Function/Structure by reading adjacent sentences, highlighting links, and annotating the role; and handle Dual Texts by locating the claim in Text 1 and Text 2’s stance on it, then eliminating Opposite/Extreme traps. Use POOD if an item looks time-heavy.

3) Key Insights (what to internalize)
Write your own word before looking at vocab choices—this locks your compass and speeds POE.


Function lives in context: read the sentence and the one before/after to see the link.


Dual Texts = claim + response: highlight both sides, then label the relationship in 3 words.


Watch for Extreme and Recycled-language traps across all three types.

Chapter 6: Reading Question Types 4–8:
Information and Ideas


1)Notes 
The 5 types (goal): Retrieval, Main Idea, Claims, Charts, Conclusions — all are evidence hunts; apply the Basic Approach with small tweaks per type.


Retrieval — “According to the text…”
Task: pull exactly what the passage says; find the line that answers and match it. Watch for Recycled/Extreme/Beyond-text traps.


Mini-playbook: Question → scan for who/what → highlight the phrase that answers → POE.


Main Idea — central idea
Task: what every sentence builds on (often first/last line, but not always). Eliminate detail-only answers (Right-Answer/Wrong-Question).


Mini-playbook: track topic across sentences → highlight the best summary line → POE.


Claims — illustrate / support / weaken
Task: underline the named claim; choose what (if true) does the job (support/weaken/illustrate). Traps: Half-Right, Opposite, Extreme/Beyond-text.


Mini-playbook: find the claim (often right before the blank) → keep only choices that fully address it.




Charts — use table/graph + passage
Task: answer must fit both the chart and the claim; biggest trap = true-about-chart but not about the claim (Right-Answer/Wrong-Question).


Mini-playbook: read title/axes/units → find claim in passage → keep only answers consistent with chart + claim; ignore distractor metrics.


Conclusions — “Which choice most logically completes the text?”
Task: pick a logical ending/summary consistent with all info. Traps: Recycled-language, detail-only (Right-Answer/Wrong-Question), Beyond-text; prefer cautious wording when evidence is suggestive (“may”).






2) Short Summary
Chapter 6 turns you into an “evidence sniper.” For Retrieval and Main Idea, highlight exactly what answers the stem and delete detail-traps. For Claims, pick the option that (if true) does the required job. For Charts, first read the title/units, then keep only answers consistent with both chart and passage claim. For Conclusions, choose the ending that fits all info—avoid recycled-word bait and prefer measured language.



3) Key Insights (what to internalize)
Line-lock: For Retrieval/Main Idea, highlight the sentence(s) your answer must match; don’t “feel” it.


Claims = job-based: Ask “Does this choice actually do what the stem asks (if true)?” not “Is it in the passage?”


Charts = two checks: Many answers fit the chart; only one also fits the claim.


Conclusion tone: Prefer cautious wording when evidence is suggestive.


Trap radar: Recycled-language ≠ correct idea; Right-Answer/Wrong-Question shows up a lot on Main Idea/Charts/Conclusions.

Chapter 7 — RW Power-Ups: Vocab Wins + Trap-Busting
1) Notes 
Why vocab matters: It shows up a lot and gets tougher if you land in a harder Module 2. Train a “Greatest Hits” list 5–10 min daily (book list + your own misses).


Endgame POE (“down to two”): When two choices remain, stop justifying—ask: “Which one is the trap?” Then eliminate by type.


The 6 traps to memorize: Opposite, Extreme Language, Recycled Language, Right-Answer/Wrong-Question, Beyond the Text, Half-Right.


How they look in practice:


Extreme = goes further than text supports (e.g., “will happen”).


Beyond the Text = sounds logical but isn’t stated/supported.


Half-Right = one correct idea + one wrong/unsupported idea.


2) Short Summary
Build daily vocab with a Greatest Hits list, and in close calls use trap IDs to eliminate the wrong answer instead of “talking yourself into” a choice.
3) Key Insights
Tiny daily vocab reps compound into higher RW accuracy.


When stuck, hunt the trap, don’t justify the survivor.


Extreme/Beyond-text phrasing is a frequent giveaway.

Chapter 8 — Poetry Without Panic + Sentence Roles That Reveal Answers
1) Notes 
Poetry = still evidence-based: You’re not grading literature; find claims/evidence and apply POE. Watch a few contractions/figurative devices—don’t over-infer.


Sentence Function = speed hack: Most non-literature passages are built from four roles: Background → Claim → Objection → Evidence. Labeling roles helps you locate main ideas and links fast.


Worked pattern (suburban-pools example): They walk through sentences showing Background, Objection (“However…”), Evidence, Claim, then Evidence. Use signal words like however, posit, found.


Why it pays: If you can tag roles, you can jump to the sentence that answers Purpose / Dual Texts / Main Idea / Claims / Conclusions.


2) Short Summary
Treat poems like any other short text: find the claim/evidence and avoid speculation. In informational passages, tag sentence roles (Background/Claim/Objection/Evidence) to zero-in on what a question needs—especially for Purpose, Claims, Charts, and Conclusions.
3) Key Insights
Poetry success = type → proof → POE (not literary intuition).


Role-tagging sentences is a map to main ideas and links.


“However” often marks an Objection; “posited” flags a Claim; dense details are often Evidence.


4) Flashcards
Q: Four common sentence roles? A: Background, Claim, Objection, Evidence.


Q: Quick poetry plan? A: Identify question type → find claim/evidence → POE traps.


Q: Word that signals an Objection? A: “However.”


Q: Why role-tagging helps Purpose/Main Idea? A: It sends you to the exact sentence that carries the idea.

Chapter 12 — Glue vs. Glitter: Specifying Info vs Extra Info
1) Notes 
Specifying vs Extra (core idea):


Specifying tells you exactly which person/thing; it stays attached—no commas. Test it by removing the phrase: if meaning/identity breaks, it’s Specifying.


Extra adds removable detail; box it off with matching commas/dashes/parentheses (one mark only at the start/end of a sentence).


Fast tests that score points:


that = Specifying → no commas (and never after a comma).


who = people, which = things. Using which for a person is wrong.


Prepositional phrase (in/of/for/by/with/on/to): usually no commas after the noun; but if it starts the sentence, use a comma.


Transitions (however, moreover, therefore): comma after at the start; commas around in the middle (they’re Extra).


Titles/labels before names:


No comma after a label before a proper name (e.g., Astronaut Mae Jemison).


But “a/an + noun” as a description is Extra → needs commas (or dashes/parentheses).


Match the punctuation set: If you already see a comma/dash before a middle chunk that is Extra, your choice must complete the pair.


How to crack these (3 steps):
 Look at choices → decide Specifying vs Extra → POE (eliminate choices that misuse who/which/that or don’t match the punctuation pair).



2) Short Summary
Decide whether a describing phrase is Specifying (identifies which → no commas) or Extra (removable detail → matching commas/dashes/parentheses). Use quick tells: that = no commas, who for people / which for things, prepositional phrase at start → comma, labels before names → no comma. If punctuation appears before an Extra chunk, finish the pair.

3) Key Insights (memorize these)
Remove-it test: If removing the phrase keeps the same subject/thing, it’s Extra → needs a matching pair.


“that” never floats after a comma. If a comma precedes it, it’s wrong.


Labels act like adjectives: Scientist Bert Vogelstein → no comma between the label and name.


Intro prepositional phrase? Follow with a comma; elsewhere, usually none.

Chapter 13 — Punctuation Power: Join Full Sentences Like a Pro
1) Notes 
Independent clause (IC): a complete sentence (subject + main verb) that can stand alone.


3 correct ways to join IC + IC:


Semicolon (;) — use anywhere a period could work.


Comma + FANBOYS (For/And/Nor/But/Or/Yet/So) — you need both the comma and the conjunction.


Colon (:) or single dash (—) — only if clause 2 explains/defines/elaborates clause 1, and clause 1 is a full IC.


3 wrong ways (auto-eliminate):
 • just a comma (, splice) · • FANBOYS without the comma · • no punctuation between two ICs.


Classic colon trap: don’t write “are: list.” Make the lead-in a full IC: “I have three priorities: …”


Transitions (however/therefore/for example): to connect two ICs, use period/semicolon before the transition and comma after it: “Sales fell; however, margins improved.”


Quick solve script (Rules items):


Look at answers → what’s changing? (semicolon, comma+FANBOYS, colon/dash, transition placement)


Check if you truly have IC + IC and whether clause 2 explains clause 1.


POE → kill comma splices, lone FANBOYS, and any colon/dash with a non-IC before it.



2) Short Summary
When two full sentences meet, join them with ;, , + FANBOYS, or : / — (only if the second explains the first and the lead-in is a full IC). Avoid comma splices and lone FANBOYS. With however/therefore, put a period/semicolon before and a comma after the transition. Solve by spotting what’s changing, testing for IC + IC, and eliminating rule-breakers.

3) Key Insights (memorize these)
Semicolon = period-strength joiner. If a period works, “;” does too.


FANBOYS needs a comma. “…, but …” ✅ vs “... but …” ❌ when both sides are ICs.


Colon/dash need a full lead-in. The first clause must be a complete sentence.


Conjunctive adverbs ≠ conjunctions. “however/therefore/for example” cannot replace FANBOYS; use .; however, … not “, however,” to join ICs.


POE speed: If you spot a comma splice or “are: list” lead-in, delete instantly.



4) Flashcards (ready to use)
Q: Three correct ways to join IC + IC?
 A: ; · , + FANBOYS · : / — (only if clause 2 explains clause 1).


Q: What must come before a colon or single dash used to join?
 A: A full independent clause.


Q: Fix: “We finished the trial, we posted the data.”
 A: “We finished the trial; we posted the data.” (or) “We finished the trial, and we posted the data.”

 A: Period/semicolon before, comma after → “…, however, …”.


Q: Which is a comma splice?
 A: “The test ended, students left.” (two ICs joined by just a comma)


Q: Choose the only okay standalone joiner:
 A: Semicolon. (FANBOYS needs a comma; transitions need . or ; before)

Chapter 14 — Join with Care: Dependent Clauses, Lists & Zero-Punctuation Rules
1) Notes
Dependent vs. Independent


Independent clause (IC) = full sentence. Dependent clause (DC) = has a subject+verb but starts with a subordinator (because, since, if, though, when, that), so it can’t stand alone.


How to join IC + DC (only two legal ways)


You may write IC + DC (usually no comma) or DC + IC (comma). These are the only punctuation patterns you’ll use for IC+DC joins. Examples: “Because it was raining, I brought my umbrella.” / “I brought my umbrella because it was raining.”


A comma can appear when the DC comes second for contrast/pause, but College Board avoids grey-area trickery.


Lists (simple → commas; complicated → semicolons)


Simple lists (3+ items): commas between items; keep items in the same grammatical form.


Complicated lists (items already have commas): separate big items with semicolons (e.g., “Alex, a parent; Taylor, a teacher; and Ali, a community member.”).


“No-punctuation” rules (auto-delete errors)


Never put a single mark between a subject and its verb; Extra info is okay only if boxed by a matching pair.


No punctuation after a preposition (“of, by, with, to …”).


Overall: don’t add punctuation without a reason.


Answer-choice recognition cues


If you see a subordinator (because/though/when) floating near the blank, it’s IC + DC → allow comma or nothing (not : ; —).


If one list item already has a comma inside, expect semicolons between items.


If a choice puts punctuation after a preposition or between subject–verb, eliminate fast.



2) Short Summary
Identify DCs by their subordinators. Join IC + DC only with comma or nothing (depending on order/meaning); avoid colons/semicolons/dashes here. Use commas for simple lists and semicolons when list items themselves contain commas. Never drop a single mark between subject and verb or after a preposition—punctuate only with purpose.

3) Key Insights (memorize these)
IC+DC ≠ fancy marks: only comma or nothing joins them.


List detector: if an item already has a comma, upgrade separators to semicolons.


Zero-punctuation traps: a lone comma between subject–verb or after prepositions is almost always wrong.



4) Flashcards (ready to use)

 A: A subordinator like because/since/if/though/when/that.


Q: Legal punctuation to join IC + DC?
 A: Comma (DC + IC) or nothing (IC + DC).


Q: Simple list punctuation?
 A: Commas between items; maintain parallel form.


Q: Complicated list rule?
 A: Use semicolons between items that already include commas.


Q: Fast error check between subject and verb?
 A: No single mark may separate them.


Q: Can punctuation come after a preposition?
 A: No. Delete those choices.

Chapter 15 — Consistency Clinic: Verbs • Pronouns • Apostrophes • Modifiers
1) Notes
A. Verbs — number first, then tense
Find the true subject (ignore prepositional phrases: One of the photos is).


Make the verb match the subject (singular/plural) before worrying about tense.


Use time cues to pick tense:


Simple past for dated events (In 2015, the court ended).


Present perfect (has/have + V3) for past-to-present effects (Researchers have found).


Past perfect (had + V3) when one past action preceded another (By the time X began, Y had finished).


Parallel verbs share the same form in lists or with the same subject (can measure and record).


B. Pronouns — agree and be clear
it = singular, they = plural. Match the closest clear antecedent.


Avoid switching person/number mid-sentence (no random you/one/they unless the passage sets it up).


C. Apostrophes — contractions vs possession
Pronoun + ’ = contraction: it’s (it is / it has), they’re (they are).


Possessive pronouns have no apostrophe: its, their, your, whose.


Noun possession:


Singular → ’s (artist’s studio).


Regular plural in -s → s’ (artists’ studios).


Irregular plural → ’s (women’s league).


Don’t add an apostrophe unless the noun owns something.


D. Modifiers — place them right
Put the modifier next to what it describes.


After an opening -ing or descriptive phrase, the next noun must be the doer:


✔ Hoping to inspire others, the artist launched workshops.


✘ Hoping to inspire others, free workshops were launched.


E. Fast solve script for “Rules”
If answers include to / -ing forms, check if the sentence needs a main verb.


Lock subject–verb number.


Set tense from nearby time verbs/phrases.


Check pronoun agreement (it/they) and clarity.


Check apostrophes (contraction vs possession).


Fix modifier placement (especially opening phrases).


POE: cross out any option that breaks these.



2) Short Summary
This chapter is all about consistency. Make verbs agree with the real subject and choose tenses from time cues (including past perfect for “earlier past”). Keep pronouns matched to their antecedents. Use apostrophes only for contractions or possession (and know the plural rules). Place modifiers right next to what they describe, especially after opening -ing phrases. Solve quickly by scanning choices for what’s changing and applying this checklist.

3) Key Insights (memorize these)
Number beats vibe: lock subject–verb agreement before tense tweaks.


Dates = simple past; “by the time” = past perfect.


its ≠ it’s: one is possessive, the other a contraction.


Modifiers cling to their noun: the doer must come right after an opening phrase.


Parallel form = clean writing: keep verbs in the same shape in lists/paired actions.

Chapter 31 — “Type-It-Right: Cracking DSAT Math Fill-Ins”
1) Notes 
What “fill-ins” are: Math questions with no choices; you type the answer yourself. They’re mixed with MCQs and follow the same loose easy→hard flow. Use your Personal Order of Difficulty (POOD): hit your strengths first.


No penalty + always answer: There’s no guessing penalty; always enter something. Only enter one answer even if many are possible.


Input rules (know these cold):


Up to 5 characters if positive; 6 if negative (includes the minus sign).


Allowed characters: digits 0–9, -, /, . (no %, π, etc.).


Fractions or decimals are both fine—use the safer form. Reduce a fraction or convert to decimal if it won’t fit. Don’t use mixed numbers.


For long/repeating decimals, fill all spaces or round the last digit.


Omit symbols the box won’t take (e.g., type 44944, not 44,944; omit °).


Core strategies still work: Geometry Basic Approach, Plugging In, and average formula T = A·N.


Quick example reminders:


Vertex/axis of symmetry: For a factored quadratic, the vertex’s x is the midpoint of the intercepts. (Here, x = (3 + (–11))/2 = –4.)


Parallel lines: Same slope; convert to y = mx + b to read off slope (e.g., m = 3/2 = 1.5).


Weighted average: Use T = A·N per group, sum Totals and Numbers, then divide (Town A/B mean).


POOD in action: Even giant numbers (e.g., 44944) are OK if they fit; don’t get spooked.


2) Chapter summary (short)
Fill-ins are standard DSAT Math problems where you must type the answer. Success = know the input rules (character limits, allowed symbols, fraction/decimal rules), lean on familiar math tactics (Plugging In, Geometry basics, T = A·N), and use POOD to manage time. There’s no guessing penalty, so always submit an answer and double-check formatting.
3) Key insights you should take away
Master the input box rules—they’re low-effort points.


Choose fraction or decimal by what’s less error-prone; avoid mixed numbers.


Always answer something (no penalty) and only one value.


Formatting traps are real: no commas, no symbols like °, %, π.


Your usual math tools still shine: Plugging In, Geometry Basic Approach, T = A·N.

Chapter 32 Game Day & The Final Week: A Student’s Playbook
1) Notes 
Mindset: Expect real test minutes to feel faster than practice; nerves are normal—preparation keeps panic in check.


The week before: Don’t suddenly sleep 12 hours the night before—this can make you groggy. Instead, wake up early each day this week so your brain is “on” at test time.


Night-before plan: Do ~1 hour of light review or a few practice problems before dinner; avoid cramming. Then relax (“veg out”) so you’re rested.


Morning-of routine: Eat a solid breakfast and warm up with a few SAT questions to get your brain thinking analytically.


Logistics: Arrive early to the test center.


ID rules (critical): Bring acceptable ID with photo and your name (driver’s license, school photo ID, or passport). Birth certificate, credit card, Social Security card are not acceptable. Also bring your admission ticket.
